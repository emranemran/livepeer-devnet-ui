Branch: delta · ~35 min read · Re-readable

Livepeer pays for video work in ETH, but decides who is allowed to do that work using LPT. Almost every line of Solidity in this repo exists to answer one of two questions: who is in the active set this round, and how does this round's newly minted LPT and collected ETH get split between them and the people who backed them. Two currencies, two jobs. Once you see that split, the whole codebase snaps into shape.

CHAPTER 01 — THE MARKET

Livepeer is a marketplace for video encoding that uses a token to decide who gets hired.

Video streaming has an expensive, boring problem: every video you upload has to be converted into a dozen different sizes and formats so it plays on a phone, a laptop, and a TV. That conversion is called transcoding, it eats GPU time, and cloud providers charge a lot for it. Livepeer's pitch is that a global pool of people with idle GPUs will do it cheaper.

So you have a two-sided market. On one side, gateways (the code and older docs call them broadcasters or senders) have video and money. On the other, orchestrators (the code calls them transcoders) have GPUs and want to get paid. The actual video never touches the blockchain — it flows directly between the two parties over the network. This repo is only the accounting and coordination layer.

Here is the part that trips up everyone new: there are two different currencies doing two different jobs. Gateways pay for work in ETH, the ordinary currency of the Ethereum ecosystem. But the right to be picked for that work is bought with LPT, Livepeer's own token. LPT is not the payment currency. LPT is the reputation-and-security deposit. You lock it up to say "I'm serious, and I have something to lose if I cheat."

FIG. 01 — TWO CURRENCIES, TWO JOBS

Why would anyone lock up LPT? Two reasons. First, staked LPT earns a share of newly minted LPT every round — inflation rewards, paid to people who show up and participate. Second, staked LPT earns a share of the ETH fees that gateways actually pay. And because most LPT holders don't run GPUs, the protocol lets them delegate: point your LPT at an orchestrator you trust, and share in what they earn. That's Chapter 02.

For where these contracts actually run and how the go-livepeer daemon drives them, see guide 04 — it puts production and this devnet side by side.

One important piece of context before you read any file: the protocol runs on Arbitrum One, not Ethereum mainnet. Arbitrum is a "layer 2" — a faster, cheaper chain that periodically posts its results back to Ethereum for security. Livepeer moved there in 2022 because transaction fees on Ethereum made micropayments uneconomical. On Ethereum, only the LPT token contract and a bridge remain. The delta branch you're on is the live L2 code.

"The chain never sees a single frame of video. It only ever settles the question of who worked, who backed them, and who gets paid."

CHAPTER 02 — STAKE & DELEGATION

BondingManager is the biggest file in the repo because it is the whole economy.

Open contracts/bonding/BondingManager.sol and you'll find about 1,800 lines. Do not read it top to bottom. It has exactly two data structures and everything else is bookkeeping around them.

A Delegator is anyone who has locked LPT. The struct tracks bondedAmount (how much they locked), delegateAddress (who they're backing), delegatedAmount (how much others have pointed at them), and lastClaimRound (how far their earnings have been tallied up). A Transcoder is a delegator who has declared themselves open for business by calling transcoder(rewardCut, feeShare). Crucially, an orchestrator is just a delegator who delegates to themselves and then accepts delegations from others. There is no separate "operator" type.

Those two commission numbers are the deal an orchestrator offers the market, and they are confusingly asymmetric — this catches everyone:

  • rewardCut — the percentage of newly minted LPT rewards that the orchestrator keeps for themselves. Higher = worse for you as a delegator.
  • feeShare — the percentage of ETH fees the orchestrator pays out to delegators. Higher = better for you as a delegator.

Both are expressed as a numerator over MathUtils.PERC_DIVISOR, which is 1_000_000. So a rewardCut of 100000 means 10%. You will see this "percentage as a millionth" pattern everywhere in the codebase; there's also a PreciseMathUtils with a 1027 divisor used where rounding errors would compound.

FIG. 02 — WHERE ONE ROUND'S REWARD GOES

The verbs a delegator uses are bond(), unbond(), rebond() and withdrawStake(). Unbonding is deliberately slow: calling unbond() creates an UnbondingLock with a withdrawRound seven rounds (about a week) in the future. That delay is the security mechanism — it means misbehavior can be detected and punished before the culprit can pull their collateral out. rebond() cancels a pending unlock and puts the stake back to work.

Only the top 100 orchestrators by total stake are active and eligible to earn in any given round (numActiveTranscoders in deploy/migrations.config.ts; the default for fresh local deploys is 10). Keeping a sorted list of 100 candidates cheaply on-chain is genuinely hard, which is why the repo carries contracts/libraries/SortedDoublyLL.sol — a sorted doubly-linked list. It's also why half the public functions have a WithHint twin, like bondWithHint(amount, to, oldPosPrev, oldPosNext, newPosPrev, newPosNext). You're telling the contract roughly where in the list your new position belongs so it doesn't have to scan. Pass zero addresses and it figures it out itself, just for more gas.

The last thing to know: earnings are lazy. Nothing is pushed to you. The protocol records a per-round "cumulative reward factor" per orchestrator, and your balance is computed on demand by comparing the factor at your lastClaimRound to the factor now. That's what pendingStake(), pendingFees() and claimEarnings() do, and it's why EarningsPoolLIP36.sol exists. Without it, paying 100 orchestrators × thousands of delegators every round would cost more gas than the rewards are worth.

CHAPTER 03 — ROUNDS & INFLATION

The protocol has a heartbeat, and someone has to press the button.

Blockchains have no clock and no cron job. Nothing happens on a schedule; something only happens when a human or a bot sends a transaction. Livepeer works around this with rounds, and rounds are the single most important concept for reading this codebase — nearly every function is annotated with which round it belongs to.

A round is simply roundLength blocks long. On mainnet that's 5,760 blocks — and since Livepeer runs on Arbitrum, where block.number reports the Ethereum L1 block number, that works out at roughly 19–20 hours (it was a clean 24 when Ethereum blocks were 15 seconds apart). contracts/rounds/RoundsManager.sol computes currentRound() by dividing the block number by the round length — that part is automatic. But a new round doesn't do anything until someone calls initializeRound(), which is permissionless and typically done by an orchestrator's bot. That single call does three things:

  1. Records the previous block's hash as the round's randomness seed.
  2. Calls BondingManager.setCurrentRoundTotalActiveStake() — freezing the active set and its total stake for the round.
  3. Calls Minter.setCurrentRewardTokens() — deciding how much new LPT may be minted this round.

Then each active orchestrator separately calls reward() to claim their slice. Miss it and that round's inflation reward is simply gone for you and your delegators. This is why running an orchestrator is an operational job, not a passive one — and it's why the newest addition to the contract exists: setRewardCaller() lets an orchestrator nominate a second, hot-wallet address that may call rewardForTranscoder() on their behalf, so the keys controlling millions in stake don't have to sit on an always-online server.

FIG. 03 — THE ROUND LOOP

contracts/token/Minter.sol holds the money-printer. Each round it computes currentMintableTokens = totalSupply × inflation, then adjusts the inflation rate for next time via a feedback loop: if less than 50% of all LPT is currently staked, inflation ticks up to bribe more people into staking; if more than 50% is staked, it ticks down. Live values on Arbitrum are an inflation of 218500 and a step of 500, against the Minter's 109 divisor — so about 0.0219% per round, moving by 0.00005% each round.

Since the "delta" upgrade there's one more claimant. Before rewards are split with delegators, treasuryRewardCutRate (10%) is skimmed off to the protocol treasury — with a safety valve: when the treasury balance crosses treasuryBalanceCeiling (750,000 LPT) the contract sets the cut rate to zero automatically until governance turns it back on.

"Nothing in this protocol happens on a schedule. It happens because somebody paid gas to make it happen."

CHAPTER 04 — PROBABILISTIC MICROPAYMENTS

Paying a tenth of a cent on a blockchain: pay rarely, but pay big, and let the math even out.

Transcoding a video segment is worth a fraction of a cent. Writing a payment to a blockchain costs more than that. If every segment triggered an on-chain transfer, the fees would dwarf the payments. Everything in contracts/pm/ exists to solve this, and it's the most genuinely clever idea in the repo.

The trick is a lottery. Instead of paying a gateway's orchestrator $0.001 ten thousand times, the gateway sends ten thousand signed tickets, each worth $10 but with only a 1-in-10,000 chance of winning. Ninety-nine-point-nine-nine percent of tickets are worthless paper that never touch the chain. The occasional winner gets redeemed on-chain for the full $10. Over time the orchestrator receives the same expected value, but pays gas maybe once a day instead of ten thousand times.

A ticket is a struct with a faceValue (payout if it wins), a winProb (the odds), and a recipientRandHash. The winner check is one line, and it's worth reading because it explains the whole design:

// MixinTicketBrokerCore.sol
function isWinningTicket(bytes _sig, uint256 _recipientRand, uint256 _winProb)
    returns (bool)
{
    return uint256(keccak256(abi.encodePacked(_sig, _recipientRand))) < _winProb;
}

Neither side can rig it alone. The gateway's signature and the orchestrator's secret random number are hashed together. The orchestrator committed to their secret in advance by publishing only its hash (recipientRandHash), so they can't fish for a value that makes a ticket win; the gateway can't predict the secret, so they can't hand out only losers. The orchestrator can check winners instantly and privately, and only bothers the chain when there's real money to collect.

FIG. 04 — THE LIFE OF A TICKET

Two pots of ETH back the tickets. The deposit is normal spending money. The reserve is a backstop that gets tapped when a winning ticket is larger than the remaining deposit, so orchestrators aren't left holding a winner from an account that just ran dry. A gateway wanting its ETH back must call unlock() and wait out the unlockPeriod — the same "slow exit" idea as unbonding.

When a winning ticket is redeemed, the ETH doesn't go straight to the orchestrator's wallet. TicketBroker calls BondingManager.updateTranscoderWithFees(), which splits it according to that orchestrator's feeShare and credits everyone's pending balance — the same lazy accounting from Chapter 02. Delegators pull their ETH out later with withdrawFees().

CHAPTER 05 — WIRING & UPGRADES

Every contract is behind a proxy, and one registry decides what the proxies point at.

Deployed smart contract code is immutable — you cannot edit it. But a protocol handling hundreds of millions of dollars needs to ship fixes. The standard answer is the proxy pattern, and Livepeer's variant is the piece of architecture you must understand before you can navigate this repo at all.

Every user-facing contract is actually two contracts. ManagerProxy holds all the storage and never changes address — it's the address in every explorer, wallet, and subgraph. It has no logic of its own; its fallback function looks up a target address and delegatecalls into it, meaning the target's code runs against the proxy's storage. The target — deployed as BondingManagerTarget, RoundsManagerTarget and so on — holds the logic and is the thing that gets replaced on an upgrade.

What does the proxy look up against? contracts/Controller.sol, a registry mapping keccak256("BondingManager") → {address, gitCommitHash}. That commit hash is a nice touch: the chain records which source revision each deployed target came from. Upgrading the entire protocol is therefore one transaction — Controller.setContractInfo(id, newTarget, hash) — and every proxy silently starts routing to new code on the next call. The Controller is also a global pause switch, and it starts paused on deployment. That last detail will bite you locally; see the runbook below.

FIG. 05 — WHO CONTROLS WHAT

Who is allowed to call that? Two separate systems, and mixing them up is a common confusion:

PROTOCOL GOVERNANCE

Governor.sol

Owns the Controller. Upgrades are staged then executed after a delay, so the community can see a change coming. Driven by a multisig, informed by off-chain LIP votes run through Poll.sol.

TREASURY GOVERNANCE · DELTA

LivepeerGovernor.sol

A newer, fully on-chain OpenZeppelin Governor that controls only the treasury for now. Voting power comes from staked LPT via BondingVotes.sol, and delegators can override the vote their orchestrator cast.

That override behavior lives in GovernorCountingOverridable.sol and is the most Livepeer-specific piece of governance in the repo: your orchestrator votes with your stake by default, but you can reclaim your share and vote against them. BondingVotes.sol is what makes it possible — it keeps historical checkpoints of everyone's stake so a vote can be scored against a past round rather than whatever the balances happen to be today.

The rest of the repo map, briefly: deploy/ holds hardhat-deploy scripts and, importantly, migrations.config.ts — the per-network parameter table (round length, active set size, inflation) worth reading in full. tasks/ holds one-off CLI commands. test/unit/ tests contracts in isolation with mocks; test/integration/ deploys the real system and drives it end to end — those are the best-written documentation in the repo. deployments/ records what is actually live on-chain.

LOCAL SETUP

Standing up your own Livepeer, start to finish.

This is the baseline: a complete, private copy of the protocol running on your laptop, with fake LPT you can mint yourself and rounds you can fast-forward at will. Nothing here touches a real network and nothing costs money. Every command below was run against this repo on Node 22 and works — including the three places the README doesn't warn you about.

git clone https://github.com/livepeer/protocol.git
cd protocol
cp .env.sample .env            # leave it empty for local work
yarn
yarn compile                   # ~1 min, downloads solc 0.8.9

The .env keys (PRIVATE_KEY, INFURA_KEY, ETHERSCAN_API_KEY) are only needed to touch real networks. For local work an empty file is fine — but the file must exist.

Run the tests first. This is the highest-value thing you can do on day one. Unit tests are fast and hermetic; integration tests deploy the entire protocol into an in-process chain and drive it end to end.

yarn test:unit          # 769 tests, ~2 min — contracts in isolation, with mocks
yarn test:integration   # full system: bond → round → reward → tickets
yarn test               # everything

Now the persistent local network. The tests spin up a throwaway chain each run; this gives you one that stays alive so you can poke at it from a console. In terminal one — and note the flag, because without it this command fails:

npx hardhat node --no-deploy    # local chain on http://127.0.0.1:8545

Gotcha #1. Plain npx hardhat node dies with HH604: No deployment found for: Controller. The hardhat-deploy plugin auto-runs every script in deploy/ on startup, and deploy_ai_service_registry.ts assumes a Controller already exists. --no-deploy skips that; you'll deploy explicitly in a moment.

In terminal two, deploy the protocol, add one dummy contract, and unpause:

yarn deploy --network localhost   # = hardhat deploy --tags Contracts,Poll
npx hardhat deploy --tags ARBITRUM_LPT_DUMMIES --network localhost
npx hardhat unpause --network localhost

Gotcha #2. The Controller's constructor sets paused = true and the deploy script never turns it off. Until you unpause, nearly every call reverts with "system is paused".

Gotcha #3 is the one that will genuinely stump you. Skip the ARBITRUM_LPT_DUMMIES line and initializeRound() reverts with the deeply unhelpful "function call to a non-contract account". Reason: the Minter computes total supply as L2 supply plus L1 circulating supply, and asks a contract called L2LPTDataCache for that second number. On Arbitrum it's real; locally nothing registers it, so the Minter calls address zero. That tag deploys a stub. (The integration tests dodge this differently — test/helpers/setupIntegrationTest.ts registers a GenericMock for the same slot.)

Two things are quietly different about a local deploy, and both are deliberate gifts to you. Because localhost isn't in the LIVE_NETWORKS list in deploy/deploy_contracts.ts, you get AdjustableRoundsManager instead of the real one — it overrides blockNum() so you can call mineBlocks(n) and skip through rounds instantly instead of waiting 5,760 real blocks. And because it isn't a production network, a LivepeerTokenFaucet is deployed holding 6,343,700 fake LPT, with another 500,000 minted straight to your deployer account. Free money, by design.

Now drive it. Put this in scripts/drive.ts — it must live inside the repo or TypeScript won't resolve hardhat — and run it with npx hardhat run --network localhost scripts/drive.ts:

import {ethers, deployments} from "hardhat"

async function main() {
    const [me] = await ethers.getSigners()
    const at = async (n: string, as = n) =>
        ethers.getContractAt(as, (await deployments.get(n)).address)

    const bm = await at("BondingManager")
    const rm = await at("AdjustableRoundsManager")
    const token = await at("LivepeerToken")

    // get the clock off block zero, then open the first round
    const roundLength = await rm.roundLength()
    await (await rm.mineBlocks(roundLength.mul(1000))).wait()
    await (await rm.initializeRound()).wait()

    // become an orchestrator: approve, self-bond, set commission
    const amount = ethers.utils.parseEther("1000")
    await (await token.approve(bm.address, amount)).wait()
    await (await bm.bond(amount, me.address)).wait()
    await (await bm.transcoder(100000, 500000)).wait()  // 10% cut, 50% feeShare

    // a transcoder only joins the active set from the NEXT round
    await (await rm.mineBlocks(roundLength)).wait()
    await (await rm.initializeRound()).wait()

    const before = await bm.transcoderTotalStake(me.address)
    await (await bm.reward()).wait()
    const after = await bm.transcoderTotalStake(me.address)
    console.log("minted:", ethers.utils.formatEther(after.sub(before)))
}

main().catch(e => { console.error(e); process.exit(1) })

That prints minted: 0.9786491 — one round of inflation on a 1,000 LPT self-stake with nobody else competing. Two details worth internalizing, because both are real protocol behavior and not local quirks: the mineBlocks(roundLength × 1000) at the top is needed because AdjustableRoundsManager starts at block zero and initializeRound() reads blockhash(blockNum − 1), which underflows; and you must cross a round boundary after registering before reward() works, because the active set is frozen when a round opens.

If you'd rather read than type: test/integration/Rewards.js is that same sequence with real assertions. When you want a shared devnet on a real testnet rather than localhost, doc/devnet.md covers it — and doc/deploy_delta.md is a genuinely interesting read: the real eleven-step checklist used to ship the last mainnet upgrade, governance calls and all.

HANDS-ON

Six things to build, shortest first.

Each one forces you to use a concept rather than read about it. Do them in order; each assumes the previous.

PROJECT 01~30 min · concept: WIRING

Get green tests and one deliberate red one.

Run yarn test:unit until it passes. Then open test/unit/BondingManager.js, change one expected number, and watch it fail. Trivial, but it proves your toolchain works and teaches you to read Hardhat's failure output — which you'll be staring at a lot.

deliverable: a passing suite, and a screenshot of one failure you caused on purpose
PROJECT 02~1 hour · concept: ROUNDS

Drive a live local protocol by hand.

Follow the runbook: node, deploy, dummies, unpause, then the drive.ts script. Get to minted: 0.9786491. Then change one thing — bond a different amount, or add a second orchestrator with a bigger stake — and predict the new number before you run it. This is the moment the protocol stops being abstract.

deliverable: a transcript showing your stake grow after reward(), plus one correct prediction
PROJECT 03~2 hours · concept: STAKE

Script the delegator's-eye view.

Write a standalone script that funds three accounts from the faucet, has them all delegate to your orchestrator with different amounts, advances ten rounds calling reward() each time, then prints each account's pendingStake(). Vary rewardCut between runs and watch the split move. You now have the skeleton of an economic simulation.

deliverable: scripts/sim-rewards.ts printing a per-delegator earnings table
PROJECT 04~2 hours · concept: MICROPAYMENTS

Win the lottery on purpose.

Using test/helpers/ticket.js as your reference, fund a deposit and reserve, construct and sign a ticket with winProb set to the maximum so it always wins, and redeem it. Then set a realistic probability and loop until one actually hits. Confirm the fees land in the orchestrator's pending balance, not their wallet.

deliverable: a test that redeems a winning ticket and asserts the fee split
PROJECT 05~half a day · concept: UPGRADES

Ship an upgrade to your own chain.

Add a trivial view function to BondingManager, compile, deploy it as a new target, and re-point the Controller with setContractInfo. Call your new function through the unchanged proxy address. Then confirm that all existing stake survived. Do this once and the proxy pattern will never confuse you again.

deliverable: a hardhat task that upgrades a target and proves state persisted
PROJECT 06weekend · concept: ECONOMICS

Answer an economics question two ways.

Pick a question like "if an orchestrator does 2% of network work, how do their rewards look as fees grow 10%/month?" and answer it twice: once as a spreadsheet built from the formulas in Chapters 02 and 03, and once as a scripted run over simulated rounds on a local chain. Compare. Where they agree, the simulation was unnecessary; where they diverge, you have found a question that genuinely needs one.

deliverable: one chart, plus a short note on which questions genuinely need a simulator

Quick reference.

LPT
Livepeer Token. Staked to earn the right to do work and to earn rewards. Not used to pay for transcoding.
Orchestrator / Transcoder
Same thing. The code says "transcoder"; docs and people say "orchestrator." Runs GPUs, self-stakes, accepts delegation.
Gateway / Broadcaster
Also the same thing, and called "sender" inside TicketBroker. Has video, pays ETH.
Delegator
Anyone who bonds LPT. Points it at an orchestrator and shares their rewards and fees.
Round
5,760 Ethereum L1 blocks, roughly 19–20 hours. The unit of time for the entire protocol. Must be opened by calling initializeRound().
Active set
The top 100 orchestrators by total stake. Only they can earn in a round; the set is frozen when the round opens.
rewardCut / feeShare
An orchestrator's terms. rewardCut is the LPT they keep; feeShare is the ETH they give away. Asymmetric on purpose — read carefully.
Unbonding period
Seven rounds you must wait between unbond() and withdrawStake(). A security delay, not a fee.
PM / probabilistic micropayment
The ticket lottery in contracts/pm/. Rare large on-chain payouts standing in for constant tiny ones.
Deposit vs reserve
A gateway's two pots of ETH. Deposit pays tickets; reserve is the backstop when a winner exceeds the deposit.
Proxy / target
Storage lives in the proxy (permanent address); logic lives in the target (replaceable). delegatecall joins them.
Controller
The registry mapping contract names to target addresses, plus the global pause switch. Upgrades happen here.
PERC_DIVISOR
1,000,000 in MathUtils (109 in the Minter's V2, 1027 in PreciseMathUtils). Percentages are numerators over it.
LIP
Livepeer Improvement Proposal. The spec-and-vote process; lipUpgradeRound in RoundsManager records when each took effect.
