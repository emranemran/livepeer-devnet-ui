Livepeer pays for video encoding in ETH, but decides who is allowed to do that work using its own token, LPT. This tool puts a real copy of that protocol on your laptop with fake money and a clock you can fast-forward — so you can stake, earn, change the rules, and break things, and the worst outcome is you press RESET.

CHAPTER 01 — WHAT YOU'RE LOOKING AT

A marketplace for video encoding, with a token deciding who gets hired.

Video streaming has a boring, expensive problem: every uploaded video must be converted into a dozen sizes and formats so it plays on a phone, a laptop and a TV. That conversion is called transcoding, it burns GPU time, and cloud providers charge a lot for it. Livepeer's pitch is that a global pool of people with idle GPUs will do it cheaper.

So there are two sides. Gateways have video and money. Orchestrators have GPUs and want paying. The video itself never touches the blockchain — it moves directly between the two over the network. The contracts you're about to run are only the accounting and coordination layer.

The bit that confuses everyone: two currencies doing two different jobs. Gateways pay for work in ETH. But the right to be picked for that work is bought with LPT, Livepeer's own token, which you lock up as a kind of security deposit. Lock more, get picked more. And because most LPT holders don't run GPUs, they can delegate — point their tokens at an orchestrator they trust and share the earnings.

CHAPTER 02 — GETTING IT RUNNING

Two processes, four commands, and three of them are non-obvious.

A "local blockchain" is just an ordinary program on your laptop pretending to be Ethereum. It listens on a port, holds a table of accounts in memory, and executes any transaction you send it — instantly, free, connected to nothing. Stop it and everything evaporates. That's the sandbox.

On top of that sits this UI: a small Node server that starts the chain, deploys Livepeer onto it, and gives you buttons. In normal use you only ever run the UI, and it drives everything else.

Hand the block below to your coding agent. It points at guides/00-setup-agent.md, which has the verified commands, the expected output at each step, and a troubleshooting table.

If you'd rather do it yourself, it's four commands — and the last three are the ones nobody warns you about:

npx hardhat node --no-deploy                                        # 1
npx hardhat deploy --tags Contracts,Poll --network localhost        # 2
npx hardhat deploy --tags ARBITRUM_LPT_DUMMIES --network localhost  # 3
npx hardhat unpause --network localhost                             # 4

Step 1 needs --no-deploy or the node dies at startup with HH604. Step 3 deploys a stub that only matters locally; skip it and opening a round fails with the gloriously unhelpful function call to a non-contract account. Step 4 exists because the protocol deploys in a paused state on purpose, and the deploy script never unpauses it.

Or ignore all of that and press RESET EVERYTHING on the SETUP panel, which runs the lot in about twenty seconds. The panel shows you each command as it runs, which is rather the point — it's a command-line tutorial wearing a UI.

"The buttons aren't hiding the commands from you. They're showing you which command you would have needed."

CHAPTER 03 — TIME IS A BUTTON

The protocol has a heartbeat, and locally you control it.

Blockchains have no clock and no scheduler. Nothing happens on a timer; things happen because somebody sent a transaction. Livepeer works around this with rounds — and rounds are the single concept that unlocks the rest of the system.

A round is a fixed number of blocks: 5,760 — counted in Ethereum blocks, not Arbitrum ones, which works out at roughly 19–20 hours. But a new round doesn't do anything until somebody calls initializeRound(). That one call freezes the list of orchestrators eligible to earn, decides how much new LPT may be created, and stores a random seed. On mainnet, a bot does it. Here, it's a button.

Then each orchestrator separately calls reward to claim their slice of that round's newly minted tokens. Miss it and the tokens are simply never created for you. This is why running an orchestrator is an operational job rather than a passive investment.

FIG. 02 — THE ROUND LOOP, AS BUTTONS

The AUTO-RUN button on the TIME panel loops all three steps as fast as the chain will go. Twenty-five rounds takes about a minute, and it's how you build enough history for the charts to say anything. It's also how you'd start answering a question like "what do a delegator's returns look like over a year?"

CHAPTER 04 — THE PANELS

Six screens, each one concept.

The rail on the left is also a rough order of operations. Number keys 1–6 jump between them.

1 · SETUP

Chain lifecycle

The four commands as a checklist, each showing the real command it runs and going green when satisfied. RESET EVERYTHING rebuilds from scratch. Use it whenever things stop making sense.

2 · TIME

Rounds and the clock

Skip forward by a round, a week, a month. Open the round. Auto-run many at once. Locally you get a rounds manager whose block number is just a variable you can bump.

3 · STAKE

Bonding and rewards

Bond LPT, delegate it, register as an orchestrator, call reward. Charts show stake over rounds and who holds the weight. This is where the economics live.

4 · PAYMENTS

The ticket lottery

Sign a batch of tickets at 1-in-N odds, see which won, redeem the winners on-chain. The convergence chart shows realized winners chasing the expectation.

5 · KNOBS

Protocol parameters

Every governance-owned dial with its units, range and consequences spelled out. Includes the inflation thermostat visualised as input and output.

6 · ACCOUNTS

Who you are

All 250 test accounts with balances and roles. Switch who you're acting as, or fund five wallets at once so you have delegators to play with.

Three things run across every panel, and they're the reason this exists rather than a pile of scripts:

  • Every input has a ? explaining what it means, what units it's in, and what breaks if you change it. Percentages are the big one — they're stored as numerators over a divisor that differs by contract, so the UI always shows you both (100000 · 10% over 1e6).
  • Every action has "show me the command" — the exact line of code the button is standing in for. That's the bridge back to the command line.
  • Every failure is translated. Contracts revert with terse strings; the UI pairs each known one with a plain sentence saying what to do about it.

And along the bottom sits the event tape: every command, transaction, gas cost and state change in order. Nothing happens invisibly.

CHAPTER 05 — WHAT'S WORTH TRYING

You control every account, so you can play all the roles at once.

This is the part that makes a local chain worth having. You aren't one participant in someone else's network — you're all of them. You can be five competing orchestrators, a dozen delegators, and the gateway paying them, simultaneously, and skip a year of protocol history while you're at it.

To understand…Do this
How commission actually affects returnsRegister two orchestrators with different reward cuts, delegate to both, auto-run 50 rounds, compare.
Why the active set mattersRegister more orchestrators than there are slots and let them compete on stake — the bottom ones fall out. (The slot count only turns one way: you can raise it, never lower it.)
The inflation thermostatMove the target bonding rate, auto-run twenty rounds, and watch inflation chase it on the two KNOBS charts.
Why payments are a lotterySign 200 tickets at 1-in-20 on PAYMENTS. Only the winners ever become transactions — that's the whole saving.
Why exits are slowUnbond, try to withdraw immediately, read the revert, then advance seven rounds and try again.
What "paused" meansPress pause on KNOBS and then try to do anything at all.

One honest caveat about the numbers. The local defaults are not mainnet's. The devnet sets a target bonding rate of 500000 where Arbitrum mainnet uses 500000000 — and since the Minter reads both against a 109 divisor, the local target lands at 0.05% instead of 50%. The KNOBS panel says so on screen and tells you what to type to fix it. Worth knowing before you conclude something strange about the economics.

HANDS-ON

Five things to do, shortest first.

01~15 min · concept: SETUP

Get to a green status bar.

Run the setup, press RESET EVERYTHING, and wait for CHAIN UP · PROTOCOL READY · LIVE. Then read the event tape from the top — you just watched a blockchain be born and 21 contracts land on it.

deliverable: three green chips
02~10 min · concept: STAKE

Earn your first tokens.

Bond 1,000 LPT to yourself, register as an orchestrator, advance a round, call reward. The tape should read stake +0.9786491 LPT. Before you press reward, open "show me the command" and read what it's about to run.

deliverable: a stake that grew on its own
03~20 min · concept: DELEGATION

Build a small economy.

Fund five accounts. Register a second orchestrator with a much lower reward cut. Delegate different amounts to each. Auto-run 25 rounds and read the stake chart — the shape of that chart is the entire argument about commission.

deliverable: two diverging lines and an opinion about why
04~20 min · concept: PAYMENTS

Win the lottery.

Fund a deposit and reserve, sign 200 tickets at 1-in-20, redeem the winners. Then do it again at 1-in-2 and at 1-in-200 and watch the convergence chart. Note how few transactions the chain actually saw.

deliverable: ETH that moved without 200 transactions
05~30 min · concept: KNOBS

Break something on purpose.

Set the unbonding period to 0 and withdraw instantly. Try to shrink the active set and read why you can't. Pause the protocol and try to bond. Each revert message is the protocol explaining one of its own rules — and the UI translates each one into a sentence.

deliverable: four reverts you can explain to someone else

Quick reference.

LPT
Livepeer's token. Staked to earn the right to do work. Not what customers pay with.
Orchestrator / Transcoder
Same thing. The code says "transcoder"; people say "orchestrator". Runs GPUs, self-stakes, accepts delegation.
Gateway / Broadcaster
Also the same thing. Has video, pays ETH.
Delegator
Anyone who locks LPT and points it at an orchestrator to share their earnings.
Bond / unbond
Locking and unlocking stake. Unbonding takes seven rounds — a deliberate security delay.
Round
5,760 Ethereum L1 blocks, roughly 19–20 hours. The unit of time for everything. Must be opened by someone calling initializeRound().
Active set
The top N orchestrators by stake. Only they earn, and the list is frozen when a round opens.
Reward cut / fee share
An orchestrator's terms. Reward cut is the LPT they keep; fee share is the ETH they give away. Opposite directions — read carefully.
Ticket
A signed lottery entry standing in for a tiny payment. Mostly worthless, occasionally worth a lot.
Deposit vs reserve
A gateway's two pots of ETH. Deposit pays winners; reserve is the backstop when the deposit runs dry.
Revert
A failed transaction. Everything it changed is undone. Reverts are normal and informative, not crashes.
Devnet
The fake chain on your laptop. Instant, free, in memory, connected to nothing.
